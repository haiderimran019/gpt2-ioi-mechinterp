
# Conmy et al. (2023)

## Paper

**Towards Automated Circuit Discovery for Mechanistic Interpretability**

## Problem

A lot of mechanistic interpretability work involves manually finding which parts of a transformer are responsible for a particular behavior. Researchers inspect attention heads, run interventions, look at activations, and gradually build a hypothesis about the circuit.

This can work for small and simple examples, but it becomes difficult to scale. There may be thousands of possible connections between components, and manually checking all of them takes a lot of time.

The paper asks whether part of this process can be **automated**.

More specifically, the authors want to automatically find the **connections between model components that form a circuit for a particular behavior**. They focus on the connections (edges) between components rather than trying to automatically explain what every component means.

## Method

The authors first describe a general workflow for circuit discovery:

1. Choose a behavior to study.
2. Create a dataset that tests that behavior.
3. Choose a metric that measures whether the model is performing the behavior.
4. Use interventions such as activation patching to identify relevant components.
5. Investigate the connections between those components.
6. Extract a smaller computational graph, or circuit, that explains the behavior.

The main contribution of the paper is automating step 5.

They introduce **ACDC (Automatic Circuit DisCovery)**. The algorithm starts with a computational graph and tests whether individual edges are important for the chosen behavior. It uses interventions to compare the model's behavior when a connection is present versus when it is effectively removed.

If removing an edge does not significantly affect the chosen metric, ACDC can remove that edge from the proposed circuit. If removing it does affect the metric, the edge is kept.

The result is a much smaller graph containing the connections that the algorithm considers important for the behavior.

## Main Findings

ACDC was able to recover circuits that had previously been found manually.

One of the main validation experiments involved a **greater-than operation in GPT-2 Small**. ACDC recovered all **5 of the 5 component types** that had been identified in the previous manual analysis.

It selected **68 edges out of roughly 32,000 possible edges** in the model, and those selected edges were all among the edges previously identified manually.

This suggests that circuit discovery does not always have to rely entirely on a researcher manually inspecting thousands of possible connections.

The authors also compare ACDC with other approaches and discuss how different choices of metric, dataset, and algorithm affect the circuit that is discovered.

The important result for me is not just that ACDC finds a small graph. It is that the method can recover a previously studied circuit **without starting from the manually identified circuit**.

## Limitations

The main limitation is that ACDC is not a complete solution to automated mechanistic interpretability.

First, the algorithm still depends on a researcher choosing the **behavior, dataset, and metric** that define what is being investigated. ACDC does not decide what interesting behavior to study by itself.

Second, the method depends on the quality of the metric. If the metric does not capture an important part of the behavior, the resulting circuit may also miss something important.

Third, the experiments are mostly on relatively small and well-defined problems. The authors are interested in scaling mechanistic interpretability, but this paper does not demonstrate that ACDC can automatically reverse-engineer arbitrary behaviors in very large language models.

There is also a tradeoff between finding a small circuit and making sure that the circuit contains everything necessary for the behavior. A circuit can look convincing while still leaving out mechanisms that matter in some situations.

Finally, the authors describe this work as a step toward automation rather than a replacement for human mechanistic analysis. Human researchers are still needed to define the task, interpret the discovered circuit, and decide whether the resulting explanation makes sense.

## Connection to IOI

This paper connects directly to **Wang et al. (2022)** and the IOI circuit.

Wang et al. manually reverse-engineered a circuit in GPT-2 Small for **Indirect Object Identification**. They used techniques such as activation patching and path patching to identify important attention heads and connections.

Conmy et al. take a step toward automating this kind of process.

The connection is therefore:

**Wang et al. → manually discover and validate an IOI circuit**

**Conmy et al. → ask whether the discovery of such circuits can be partially automated**

The important difference is that Wang et al. were mainly trying to understand the IOI mechanism itself, while Conmy et al. are trying to develop a general method for **finding circuits**.

This makes Conmy et al. a useful follow-up paper for my project because it changes the question from:

> “Can we find a circuit?”

to:

> “Can we build a procedure that finds circuits for us?”

## My Understanding

My main takeaway from this paper is that finding a circuit by manually looking through a transformer is extremely time-consuming, so the authors try to automate part of the process.

I think of the model as a large graph. The components are nodes, and the information flowing between them forms edges.

In the Wang IOI paper, the researchers had to investigate these components and connections themselves and gradually build an explanation of how GPT-2 solves IOI.

ACDC tries to do some of this searching automatically.

A simple way I understand it is:

**Start with a large computational graph**

↓

**Test an edge**

↓

**Remove/intervene on that edge**

↓

**Check whether the model's behavior changes**

↓

**Keep important edges and remove unnecessary ones**

↓

**End up with a smaller circuit**

The part I find most interesting is that the algorithm is not trying to say:

> “This attention head means X.”

Instead, it is mainly trying to determine:

> “Is this connection between components important for the behavior I am measuring?”

That makes the method feel more concrete to me.

The paper also helped me understand something about mechanistic interpretability that I did not fully appreciate from the IOI paper: **circuit discovery itself is a research problem.** Even after we know how to inspect a model, we still have to figure out which components and connections actually belong to the mechanism.

So I see the two papers as complementary. Wang et al. show what a detailed manually discovered circuit can look like, while Conmy et al. try to make the process of finding such circuits more systematic and automated.

I don't think this paper means that circuit discovery is now solved. ACDC still depends on human choices about the behavior and metric being studied, and the resulting circuit still needs to be interpreted and validated.
